Most people read the first AI answer, decide it's a bit flat and leave. This week's idea: that first answer is a draft. In my experience the useful one is usually reply #3, after you've told the AI what's wrong with the first.

A good brief, the topic of issue 2, gets you a better first draft. It's still a draft. What turns it into something you can use is knowing what to say next, and that fits in one line.

Name the problem, then send the line

Before you reply, say in one word what's wrong with the answer. Each problem has a matching follow-up. Stay in the same chat, because the AI needs to see what it wrote, and send one or two lines at a time. Ask for five changes at once and you'll get a muddle.

The assumption question

If you keep only one, keep this: "What did you assume that I didn't tell you?"

AI fills gaps quietly. It decides how many people you're cooking for, what your budget is and which country you live in, and it doesn't mention any of it. This question drags those guesses into the open so you can correct the wrong ones.

Before

A weekly meal plan with salmon twice and a 40-minute dinner every night.

After

"I assumed you cook for two, eat fish and have about 40 minutes." You reply "One person, no fish, 20 minutes max" and get a plan you'll actually follow.

The list of assumptions is often more useful than the answer, because it shows you what you forgot to say. I ask it before acting on any plan an AI gives me.

Why reply #3 is the good one

Here's a typical run. You ask for a plan to learn some Spanish before a holiday. Reply 1 is a tidy eight-week plan that would suit anyone. You send the too-generic line. It asks your level, how much time you have each day and what you'll need Spanish for. You answer: total beginner, 15 minutes on the commute, ordering food and asking directions.

Reply 2 is built around restaurant and street phrases and sized for the commute. Good, but long. You send "Turn this into steps I can do in the next hour." Reply 3 is today's first session: ten phrases, a way to practise them out loud and a two-minute self-test. That's the one you use.

Two or three follow-ups is usually enough. If you're on reply 8 and still unhappy, the brief is the problem. Open a new chat, paste the best version so far and brief it properly.

Prompt of the week

The longer version of the assumption question. It ranks the guesses by how much they changed the answer, so you fix the big ones first.

What did you assume that I didn't tell you?

List each assumption. For each one, say whether it changed your answer a lot or a little, and what you'd change if it's wrong.

Then stop and wait for my corrections before you rewrite anything.
Heads-up

Follow-ups fix the shape of an answer, not its facts. Asking again can give you a different wrong answer, and AI tends to give way the moment you push back, even when it was right. Ask for reasons instead: "Explain why, and tell me if you still think your first answer was correct." For money, health, legal or tax questions, treat the result as a draft and check with a qualified person when it matters.
