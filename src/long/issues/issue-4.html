This one is for anyone who picks creators for a brand, a shop or a client. Follower count is the easiest number to see and the least useful one to choose on. In more than four years and 850+ creator deals on the brand side, the creators who delivered were rarely the biggest accounts. They were the best fit.

Why follower count misleads

A follower count tells you how many people pressed follow at some point. It doesn't tell you who they are, whether they still watch or whether they'd care about your product. Two numbers sitting right next to it tell you far more: average views on recent posts, and who the audience actually is.

Here's a made-up example. Creator A has 250,000 followers, gets about 12,000 views per post and charges €2,000. Creator B has 40,000 followers, gets about 15,000 views and charges €600. A costs about €167 per 1,000 views. B costs €40. If B's audience also matches your brief, the smaller account is the better buy by a wide margin.

The five-factor fit score

Score every creator from 1 to 10 on five factors, then weight them:

FactorWeightWhat to look at
Audience fit30%Country, age range, language and interests of their followers, compared with your brief
Content fit25%Would your product look natural in their last 12 posts?
Engagement health20%Real comments, steady numbers, no sudden spikes
Cost15%Rate compared with likely views: the cost per 1,000 views
Brand safety10%Past controversies, recent competitor deals, tone

Audience fit carries the most weight because a great creator with the wrong audience is still the wrong creator. Brand safety carries the least because it works more like a gate: a serious red flag rules someone out however well they score elsewhere.

For the total, multiply each score by its weight and add them up. A creator scoring 8, 7, 6, 9 and 9 gets 2.4 + 1.75 + 1.2 + 1.35 + 0.9 = 7.6 out of 10.

The weights are a starting point. A campaign where reach matters most might push Cost up. A sensitive product category might push Brand safety up. Change them per campaign, and write down why.

Prompt of the week

AI is good at the first pass: reading your notes, applying the weights the same way every time and spotting patterns across twenty creators. Give it the brief and whatever numbers you have.

Help me shortlist creators for this brief: [product, campaign goal, target audience, country, budget in €].

Score each creator from 1 to 10 on:
- audience fit (30%)
- content fit (25%)
- engagement health (20%)
- cost, based on rate per 1,000 average views (15%)
- brand safety (10%)

Show a table with each score, the weighted total out of 10, one sentence of reasoning per creator and a red-flag column. Rank them, then tell me which 2 you'd drop first and why.

Use only the information I give you. Where data is missing, write "unknown" and don't guess. Don't guess anyone's personal traits from photos or names.

Creators: [name, platform, followers, average views on last 10 posts, rate in €, audience stats they shared, notes on recent posts]

The two checks only humans can do

AI can't see a creator's private audience data, and it can't tell whether comments or followers were bought. Both need a person.

  1. Ask for the audience screenshots. Creators can see their own audience stats in their platform's analytics. You can't. Ask for screenshots of the countries, age ranges and recent reach, and score Audience fit from those, not from what the feed looks like.
  2. Read the comments yourself. Scroll the comments on a few recent posts. Real comments mention the content, ask questions and sometimes disagree. Warning signs: lots of emoji-only or generic praise, the same handful of accounts every time, comments that don't match the post. A score can't tell you whether the people are real. Ten minutes of reading usually can.

Then correct the AI's scores wherever you disagree. Your judgement is the final layer. The score is a way to compare, not a verdict.

Try this today

Take three creators you're considering, or three you follow if you're just practising. For each, note followers, average views on the last 10 posts and their rate if you know it. Work out cost per 1,000 views: rate ÷ average views × 1,000. Then read the comments on two recent posts each. It takes about 20 minutes. See whether your ranking still matches the one follower count gave you.
