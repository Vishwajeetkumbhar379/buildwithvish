Every week someone posts that one AI "destroys" another. Then you open all three, get three similar answers, and wonder why you're paying for any of them. This page is for anyone choosing between ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini, or deciding whether to add Copilot, Perplexity or Gemini Notebook. By the end you'll know which tool fits each kind of job you do, what the free plans already cover, and you'll have run your own 20-minute test with the same prompts in each tool. Reading takes about 15 minutes. The test takes 20.

In short
  • All three main assistants are good enough for everyday writing, explaining and planning. The differences show up in specific jobs, not in general chat.
  • Rough split: Claude for long writing, documents and building small tools; ChatGPT for images, voice and an all-round app; Gemini if your life runs on Gmail, Docs and Drive.
  • Copilot makes most sense if you already pay for Microsoft 365. Perplexity is a search engine with answers. Gemini Notebook is for studying your own sources.
  • Don't trust anyone's ranking, including mine. Run the same three real tasks in each tool and score them.
  • Pay for one tool you'll learn properly. Keep a second free one as a cross-checker.

Before you start

You need free accounts with the tools you want to compare. Sign up with the same email for each so it's easy to keep track. ChatGPT is at chatgpt.com, Claude at claude.ai and Gemini at gemini.google.com (it uses your Google account). You don't need to pay for anything to follow this page.

Also have three real tasks ready from your own week. Not "write a poem about cats". Something you'd genuinely do: an email you need to send, a document you need to understand, a decision you're stuck on. Real tasks show differences that toy tasks hide.

One rule before we compare anything: the "best" tool changes every few months as the companies release new models. A tool that fits how you work stays useful much longer than whichever one is top of a leaderboard this week.

The main tools in one minute each

Here's what each one is, in plain words. A "model" is the AI brain behind the app. Each company has several, and the paid plans give you the stronger ones.

Claude (Anthropic)

Claude is strong at reading and writing long things: reports, contracts, a 60-page PDF, a brief that needs to keep one voice from start to finish. It also builds things you can use straight away, such as a small calculator, an interactive checklist, a one-page website or a real Excel or PowerPoint file. Its Projects keep files and instructions together for ongoing work. One clear gap: Claude doesn't generate photos or illustrations the way image tools do. It makes charts, diagrams and designs instead. Full walkthrough: Claude from zero.

ChatGPT (OpenAI)

ChatGPT is the all-rounder most people try first. It's good at everyday chat, has strong image creation and editing, a polished voice mode (in the mobile app you can even share your camera or screen during a voice chat), study mode for learning, and a "Work" mode on paid plans that builds documents and decks for you. Free users get unlimited everyday text chats, though Free and Go accounts may see ads. Full walkthrough: ChatGPT from zero.

Gemini (Google)

Gemini's advantage is where it lives. It connects to Gmail, Calendar, Drive and other Google apps, and paid Google AI plans put it inside Gmail and Docs. The free plan is generous: image generation and editing, Deep Research, Gemini Live (spoken conversation, with camera and screen sharing on your phone) and Canvas are all listed as free. Paid plans also come with extra Google storage, which matters if your Drive is nearly full. Full walkthrough: Gemini for people who live in Google.

The supporting cast

  • Microsoft Copilot has a limited free app. Microsoft 365 Personal, Family and Premium subscriptions add Copilot inside Word, Excel, PowerPoint and Outlook. On family plans, only the subscription owner gets the AI features.
  • Perplexity is a search engine that answers in sentences and cites its sources. Good for quick fact-finding where you want the links in front of you.
  • Gemini Notebook (called NotebookLM until July 2026) answers questions using only the sources you give it: PDFs, Google Docs, websites, YouTube videos. It can also turn them into an audio overview. Brilliant for studying or getting on top of a pile of documents.

Job by job: the reasoning

Writing

For a short email, any of the three will do. The difference shows on long pieces: a ten-page report, a proposal, a set of creator briefs that all need the same tone. My experience is that Claude holds instructions and voice best over length, which is why I use it most for writing. ChatGPT is quicker to give you lots of variations, which suits headlines and hooks. The bigger lever, whichever you use, is the brief. A vague request gets average writing from every tool. See Brief AI the way you'd brief a pro.

Research

There are two kinds of research job. Quick facts ("what's the notice period for X in Germany?") suit Perplexity or any assistant with web search switched on. Big questions ("compare the main ways a small shop can take payments") suit a deep research mode, which runs many searches and writes a cited report in a few minutes. ChatGPT gives Free users a small deep research allowance, Gemini lists Deep Research on its free plan, and Claude's Research needs a paid plan. Whatever you use, open the sources for any number you'll repeat. All of them can misread a page or cite something out of date.

Files

All three read PDFs, Word files and photos of paper letters. For one long document you need to understand properly, I'd use Claude. For a stack of sources on one topic (course readings, a set of reports, YouTube talks) Gemini Notebook is the specialist: it answers from your sources and shows where each answer came from. On its free plan you can have up to 100 notebooks with 50 sources each.

Coding and building

If you want something that works, like a tracker, a calculator or a one-page website, Claude builds it in a side window where you can use it straight away and keep asking for changes. ChatGPT's Work mode (paid) and Codex also build things, and Gemini has Canvas. As a non-engineer, what matters is how quickly you get to a working first version and how well the tool explains its steps. Test that with the build prompt below.

Images

Easy one. ChatGPT and Gemini both create and edit images, including on their free plans, with limits. Claude doesn't make photos or illustrations, though it's good at charts, diagrams and designed pages. If images are a big part of your work, add a design tool like Canva too.

Google or Microsoft users

If your email, calendar and files are in Google, Gemini can read them through Connected Apps, which saves endless copy and paste. Claude and ChatGPT can connect to Google apps as well, but Gemini is built in. If you already pay for Microsoft 365 Personal or Family, try Copilot inside Word and Outlook before you pay for anything else.

Privacy-sensitive work

For anything confidential, the plan type matters more than the brand. On personal plans, all three use chats to improve their models unless you change a setting (Claude asks you to choose; ChatGPT and Gemini have it on by default with an off switch). Business plans are different: OpenAI says it doesn't train on Business data, and Google says chats from qualifying Workspace accounts aren't reviewed by humans or used to improve its AI models. Use the account your employer approves, and check the privacy settings in your AI apps for your personal ones.

Privacy

Whatever tool you pick, never paste passwords, ID or bank numbers, or other people's private details without their consent. Check your employer's rules before you upload work documents.

Test them yourself in 20 minutes

This is the bit that actually decides it. You'll run the same prompts in two or three tools, side by side, and score what comes back.

How to run a fair test

  1. Open each tool in its own browser tab, on its free plan, in a fresh chat.
  2. Paste exactly the same prompt into each. Don't help one tool more than another.
  3. Run one follow-up in each, the same follow-up, because the second answer matters as much as the first.
  4. Score each answer out of 5 on the scorecard below, straight away, before you forget which was which.

Use these four prompts. Replace the brackets with your own real details.

Here's a real task from my week: [describe it and paste anything it needs, e.g. "reply to this email from a client who wants a discount"].
Who it's for: [the reader and what they care about].
Write the first draft I'd actually send. Keep it under [length].
After the draft, list the three assumptions you made that I should check before sending.
I've attached [a long document, e.g. my rental contract / a report from work, with personal numbers removed].
1. Summarise it in five plain-English bullets.
2. List every date, deadline, fee or obligation, with the section it's in.
3. Quote the one clause I'd most regret not reading.
If anything is unclear in the document, say so rather than guessing.
Search the web and answer: [a question you need answered, e.g. what do I need to do to register a small side business in Germany?].
Give me the answer in five bullets, then a table of sources with the publisher and the date each was published or updated.
Prefer official sources. Mark anything you couldn't confirm.
Build me a simple tool that [does one useful thing, e.g. splits a shared bill between friends when people ordered different amounts].
It should work on my phone and fit on one screen.
Ask me up to three questions first if you need to. Then explain in two lines how to use it.

Then send the same follow-up to every tool after Prompt 1: "Make it 30% shorter and more direct, and tell me what you cut."

The scorecard

Score each out of 5What to look for
Did it do the job?Would you send or use this with small edits?
Did it listen?Length, format and tone as asked
Was it honest?Flagged guesses, admitted gaps, real sources you can open
Did the follow-up improve it?Better on the second try, not just different
Did you enjoy using it?Speed, layout, the app on your phone. This matters more than people admit.
Weak test

"Which AI is smartest?" asked in each tool. Every answer sounds fine and you learn nothing.

Better test

The client email you have to send tomorrow, pasted into each tool with the same brief, then the same follow-up. You'll know which draft you'd actually send.

Tip

Check the research answers properly. Open two sources from each tool and see whether they say what the AI claims. A tool that quietly misquotes a source is worse than one that says "I couldn't confirm this".

How to decide what to pay for

After the test, most people find one tool they prefer for their main job. Use its free plan for two weeks. Upgrade only when you hit the same limit several times a week, or you need a specific paid feature like Claude's Research, ChatGPT's Work mode or Gemini inside Gmail.

The rule I follow: pay for one, learn it properly, and keep a second free one as a fact-checker. When an answer matters, ask the second tool "What's wrong with this?" and paste the first answer in. Two tools you know well beat five you half-use.

Two situations where paying for a second tool makes sense:

  • You need images often and your main tool is Claude. Add ChatGPT or Gemini, or a design tool.
  • Your work lives in Google or Microsoft and your main tool is something else. Gemini inside Gmail, or Copilot inside Word, saves real time.
Here's an answer another AI gave me to this question: [question].
[Paste the answer.]
Check it like a careful editor. What's wrong, missing or overstated? Which claims would you want a source for? Give me a corrected version only if you're confident, and mark what you changed.

Where this goes wrong

  • Rankings go stale fast. Each company releases new models every few months. Re-run your test twice a year instead of following headlines.
  • One test isn't the whole story. A tool can lose on your email and win on your spreadsheet. Test the jobs you do most.
  • Free plans hide the paid models. If a tool feels weak on free, it may be the smaller model. Judge it on the jobs you'd actually pay for, and remember paid models are usually stronger.
  • All of them can be confidently wrong. Picking the "best" tool doesn't remove the need to check numbers, laws and sources.
  • Features differ by country and account. Some features roll out to the US first, and work accounts may have features switched off by an admin.

When it gets stuck

  • "All three answers look the same." Your task is too easy to show a difference. Use a harder real task: a long document, a tricky email, a spreadsheet.
  • "One tool refused or gave a vague answer." Add context: who you are and why you're asking. If it still won't help, that's useful information for your choice.
  • "I hit a limit halfway through the test." That's a real data point too. Note it, and finish the test the next day.
  • "I can't find a feature mentioned here." It may be on a paid plan, not in your country yet, or renamed. Search the tool's help centre for the feature name.
  • "I like two of them equally." Pick the one whose phone app you'll actually open. You'll use it more, and use beats quality.
  • "My company already gives me one." Use that for work. Test others only for personal tasks, with nothing confidential.

Questions people ask

Which AI is the best overall?

There isn't one for everybody. They're close enough on everyday tasks that fit matters more than raw ability. Choose by the jobs you do most, and test with your own tasks.

Is the free version enough?

For everyday writing, explaining and planning, often yes. You'll notice limits first on long documents, images, voice and deep research. Upgrade when one of those gets in your way every week.

Should I pay for more than one?

Usually not. One paid plan and one free backup covers most people. Add a second paid plan only if it fills a clear gap, like images or working inside Gmail or Word.

Can I move my chats or memory from one to another?

You can export your data from each of them, and Claude has an experimental option to import memory from another assistant. In practice the easiest move is to copy your custom instructions and key files into the new tool's settings or a Project.

What about other assistants?

There are plenty, and some are very good at one thing. If you're unsure which kind of tool you need at all, try the AI tool finder, then come back to this test.
