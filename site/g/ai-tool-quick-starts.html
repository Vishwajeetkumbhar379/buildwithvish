You've signed up for a new AI tool, the blank box is blinking, and you have no idea what to ask it. This page gives you one useful first task for ten popular tools, each doable in about ten minutes. For every tool you get what it's for, what's free and what costs money (checked in October 2026), a step-by-step first task with a prompt to copy, and the one trap I'd warn a friend about. Don't try all ten today. Pick the two or three that match your work, do their first task, and you'll know within half an hour whether each one earns a place.

In short
  • A new tool sticks when your first try solves a real problem. Use a real task from this week, not a test question.
  • All ten tools below have a free way in. Paid plans add limits, models and features, so start free.
  • Each first task takes about ten minutes and ends with something you can actually use.
  • Every tool has a trap, usually around limits, privacy or trusting the output too much. Read it before you start.
The plan
  1. Pick your tools Choose two or three from the cheat sheet that match your week. 3 min
  2. Get one real task ready An email, a document, a deck, a meeting. 2 min
  3. Do the first task Follow the steps and paste the prompt. 10 min per tool
  4. Keep or drop If it saved you time, set it up properly. If not, close the tab. 2 min

Checked on 6 October 2026 against each tool's own pricing and help pages. Prices are shown in the currency the company listed, before tax. AI apps change fast: if a button has moved, search the tool's help pages for the feature name.

Before you start

Three things make these first tasks work.

  • One real task. "Summarise this real email thread" teaches you more than "tell me a fun fact". You'll see how the tool handles your actual mess.
  • A free account. Sign up with the same email for each tool so you can find them again. Don't enter card details until you've done the first task.
  • A clean document. Before you upload anything, remove passwords, ID numbers, bank details and other people's private information. Check your employer's rules before you use work files in a personal account.

Not sure which tools you need at all? Read ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini? Pick by job first, or try the AI tool finder.

The assistants: Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and Copilot

These are general AI assistants. You talk to them in plain sentences and they write, explain, read files and search the web. Most people need only one of them as their main tool.

1. Claude

What it's for: long writing, reading long documents, and building small tools and real files (Word, Excel, PowerPoint). Free or paid: the free plan includes web search, uploads, memory, voice and up to five Projects. In October 2026 Pro cost $20 a month, or $17 a month billed yearly.

First task: a Project for something you do every week. A Project is a workspace that keeps files and instructions together, so you stop re-explaining yourself.

  1. Sign in at claude.ai and click "Projects" in the sidebar, then "+ New Project".
  2. Name it after a weekly job, such as "Weekly client update".
  3. Upload one document you use for that job, such as last week's update or a template.
  4. Click "Set project instructions" and paste the prompt below.
  5. Start a chat in the Project and give it this week's notes.
This Project is for [weekly job, e.g. writing my Friday update to my manager].
The file here is [what it is, e.g. last week's update, which shows the format and tone I like].
In every chat:
- Match the structure and tone of the file.
- Lead with results, then problems, then what I need from others.
- Keep it under [250] words and use British English.
- If my notes are missing something important, ask me before writing.
The trap

Putting everything into one endless chat. Long chats get muddled and use up your allowance faster. Start a new chat inside the Project each week. The files and instructions carry over. More in Claude from zero.

2. ChatGPT

What it's for: an all-round assistant with strong images, voice and study mode. Free or paid: Free gives unlimited everyday text chats and limited uploads, images, voice and deep research, and may show ads. In October 2026 Plus cost $20 a month. Go is a cheaper middle plan whose price depends on your country.

First task: teach it who you are, then talk to it.

  1. On the web, open Settings, then Personalization, and switch on "Enable customization".
  2. Paste the prompt below into your custom instructions and save.
  3. On your phone, tap the Voice icon in the message bar and talk through tomorrow's plan on a short walk.
About me: I'm [first name], I live in [country] and work as [job]. I mostly want help with [two or three things].
How to answer:
- Lead with the answer, then the reasoning. Keep it short unless I ask for more.
- Use plain British English. Explain any jargon the first time.
- If my request is unclear, ask me up to three questions first.
- Say when you're unsure. Never invent facts, prices or sources.
The trap

Files you upload are saved in your Library, so deleting a chat doesn't delete the file. If you uploaded something sensitive, delete it from Library too. Also decide on the "Improve the model for everyone" switch under Settings, then Data controls. Full setup in ChatGPT from zero.

3. Gemini

What it's for: anyone whose email, calendar and files live in Google. Free or paid: the free plan includes image creation, Deep Research, Gemini Live and 15 GB of storage. In October 2026 Google AI Pro cost €21.99 a month in Germany ($19.99 in the US), and adds Gemini inside Gmail and Docs plus 5 TB of storage.

First task: summarise your longest email thread.

  1. At gemini.google.com, click Settings & help, then Connected Apps, and check Gmail is switched on. (If you don't see Connected Apps, look under Personal Intelligence first.)
  2. Find the subject line of a long, messy thread in your inbox.
  3. Paste the prompt below with that subject line.
Look in my Gmail for the email thread with the subject "[subject line]".
Summarise it in five lines: what's been decided, what's still open, and who is waiting for what from whom.
Then draft a short reply from me that moves it forward. Don't send anything. If you find more than one matching thread, ask me which one.
The trap

Connected Apps need the "Keep Activity" setting on when you use Gemini on the web, and with Keep Activity on, Google says a subset of chats can be reviewed by trained human reviewers. Don't put anything in a Gemini chat you wouldn't want a reviewer to read. Details in Gemini for people who live in Google.

4. Microsoft Copilot

What it's for: people who already work in Word, Excel, PowerPoint and Outlook. Free or paid: there's a limited free Copilot app. In October 2026 Microsoft listed Microsoft 365 Personal at $9.99 a month in the US, which adds Copilot inside Word, Excel, PowerPoint and Outlook. On Family plans only the subscription owner gets the AI features.

First task: turn a Word document into a slide outline.

  1. Open a Word document you've written, such as a proposal or a report.
  2. Open Copilot in Word (look for the Copilot icon). If you only have the free app, paste the text into Copilot instead.
  3. Paste the prompt below.
Turn this document into an outline for a 5-slide presentation for [audience, e.g. my team's Monday meeting].
For each slide give: a title of no more than 8 words, three short bullets, and one line of speaker notes.
The last slide should list the decisions we need. Don't add facts that aren't in the document.
The trap

Training on your chats is a setting you need to check. On copilot.com it's under your profile, then Privacy, where there are separate switches for training on conversations and on voice.

Research and learning: Perplexity and Gemini Notebook

5. Perplexity

What it's for: looking things up when you want the answer and the sources together. Free or paid: the free plan gives cited search answers with basic models and limited daily use. In October 2026 Pro cost $20 a month.

First task: answer one real question and check every source.

  1. Go to perplexity.ai and paste the prompt below with your question.
  2. Open every source it cites, one by one.
  3. For each, check: does the page actually say what the answer claims?
[Your question, e.g. What are the rules for cancelling a mobile phone contract in Germany?]
Answer in five short bullets, each with the source it came from.
Prefer official and primary sources. Tell me the date each source was published or updated, and say clearly if sources disagree or if you couldn't find a reliable answer.
The trap

A citation is not proof. Sometimes the linked page says something slightly different, or is years old. That's why the first task is opening the sources. Make it a habit.

6. Gemini Notebook (formerly NotebookLM)

What it's for: understanding a pile of your own sources. It answers from what you give it and shows where each answer came from. Google renamed NotebookLM to Gemini Notebook in July 2026, and it still lives at notebooklm.google. Free or paid: the free plan allows up to 100 notebooks with 50 sources each. Paid Google AI plans raise those limits, as Google's plans help page shows.

First task: a study notebook on one topic.

  1. Create a new notebook and select "Add sources".
  2. Add three sources on one topic: PDFs, Google Docs, web links or YouTube videos all work.
  3. Ask the prompt below in the chat.
  4. If you have a commute coming up, generate an audio overview and listen to it.
Using only these sources, make me a study guide on [topic].
1. The five most important ideas, each in two sentences, with a citation.
2. Any point where the sources disagree.
3. Ten quiz questions, from easy to hard, with answers at the end.
If something I'd expect to find isn't in the sources, tell me rather than filling the gap.
The trap

It's only as good as what you feed it. Three weak blog posts give you a confident summary of weak blog posts. Add the best sources you can find, and click the citations for anything you'll rely on.

Making things: Canva and Gamma

7. Canva AI

What it's for: designs, social posts, presentations and image edits, with Canva's templates around them. Free or paid: in October 2026 Canva's help pages said Free users get up to 20 uses a month of its AI tools, while Pro has unlimited standard AI tools and more uses of the premium ones. Canva's US pricing page listed Pro at US$144 a year. Check your local price at checkout. The limits are on Canva's AI usage help page.

First task: turn one paragraph into a carousel.

  1. On the Canva homepage, select "Canva AI" in the side navigation.
  2. Choose "Design" as the content type.
  3. Paste the prompt below with your paragraph, then edit the result in the normal Canva editor.
Turn this paragraph into a 6-slide square carousel for [platform, e.g. LinkedIn].
Slide 1: a short hook question. Slides 2 to 5: one idea each, under 20 words. Slide 6: one question for the reader.
Style: [e.g. calm, lots of white space, dark green and cream]. Large, readable text on a phone.
Paragraph: [paste your paragraph]
The trap

Twenty AI uses a month go quickly if you keep regenerating. Get one decent version, then edit it by hand in the editor, which doesn't use AI credits. Always proofread the text on every slide. For the full workflow, see Make a LinkedIn carousel with AI in 45 minutes.

8. Gamma

What it's for: turning an outline or notes into a first-draft presentation, document or simple web page. Free or paid: in October 2026 new free users got 400 credits at sign-up, which don't refresh, and could create up to 10 slides per prompt, with Gamma branding on the result. Paid plans (Plus, Pro, Ultra) refresh credits monthly and remove the branding. Prices are on Gamma's pricing page, and the credit rules are on its credits help page.

First task: a first-draft deck from your outline.

  1. Sign up at gamma.app and choose to create a new presentation from text.
  2. Paste the prompt below with your outline.
  3. If Gamma shows you an outline first, check it before it builds the slides, then generate.
  4. Export as PDF, PowerPoint or Google Slides if you want to finish it elsewhere.
Create a 7-slide presentation for [audience] about [topic]. The goal: [what the audience should decide or do afterwards].
Use this outline and don't add claims that aren't in it:
[paste your outline]
One idea per slide, short headlines, no more than four bullets per slide, and a final slide with next steps. Keep the design simple and professional.
The trap

Free credits are a one-off pot. Don't regenerate a whole deck to fix one slide. Edit the slide directly. And check every number and claim: a deck that looks finished is easy to present before anyone has checked it.

Automating and meetings: Zapier and a meeting note-taker

9. Zapier

What it's for: connecting apps so one thing happens automatically after another, without code. Each automation is called a Zap. Free or paid: in October 2026 the free plan included 100 tasks a month (a task is one action Zapier completes) and two-step Zaps, plus Zapier's Copilot assistant in beta. The Professional plan started at $19.99 a month, according to Zapier's pricing page.

First task: form entry to spreadsheet row.

  1. Create a simple Google Form (name, email, message) and a Google Sheet with matching columns.
  2. In Zapier, start a new Zap and describe it to Copilot with the prompt below.
  3. Connect your Google account when asked, and map each form field to a column.
  4. Submit a test entry with fake data and check the row appears. Then switch the Zap on.
When someone submits my Google Form "[form name]", add a new row to my Google Sheet "[sheet name]" with these columns: date submitted, name, email, message.
Keep it to two steps. Tell me which fields you mapped to which columns so I can check them before I turn it on.
The trap

A Zap runs on its own once it's on. Test with fake data first, and check what access you're giving Zapier when you connect each account. For a bigger example, see Automate meeting notes into tasks with Zapier.

10. An AI meeting note-taker (Otter.ai as the example)

What it's for: recording and transcribing meetings, then summarising them into notes and actions. There are many of these. I've used Otter.ai as the example because its free plan is clear. Free or paid: in October 2026 Otter's free Basic plan gave 300 transcription minutes a month, up to 30 minutes per conversation, and could join Zoom, Microsoft Teams and Google Meet. Pro cost $8.33 per user a month billed yearly, according to Otter's pricing page.

First task: notes and actions from one real meeting.

  1. Connect your calendar so the assistant knows about your meetings, or start a recording manually.
  2. Tell everyone in the meeting at the start that you're using an AI note-taker, and check they're OK with it.
  3. After the meeting, open the transcript and paste it into your main assistant (or the note-taker's own chat, if it has one) with the prompt below.
From this meeting transcript, give me:
1. A summary in five bullets.
2. A table of actions with: task, owner, deadline. Leave the deadline blank if nobody said one.
3. Decisions made, in one line each.
4. Anything that sounded agreed but wasn't clearly confirmed.
Use only what was said. Don't guess names or dates.
The trap

Consent. In Germany and many other countries, recording people without their agreement can be illegal, and many companies ban note-takers in some meetings. Always ask first, and don't use one in confidential meetings unless your employer allows it. This is general information, not legal advice. If you're unsure, ask your company's legal or data protection contact.

Where quick starts go wrong

A first task shows you whether a tool is worth more of your time. It doesn't show everything. A few honest limits:

  • Free plans show the smaller version. Limits, slower models and missing features can make a tool look weaker than it is. If it nearly did the job, it may be worth a month's paid trial for the specific job you need.
  • AI output looks finished before it is. Decks, designs and summaries look polished on the first try, which makes it easy to skip checking. Every tool here can get a fact, a name or a number wrong.
  • Tools overlap. Your main assistant can already draft slides, summarise meetings and do research. Add a specialist tool only when it's clearly better or faster for a job you do often.
  • Each new account is more of your data in more places. Delete accounts you don't keep, and don't connect your email to tools you're only trying.

When it gets stuck

  • "I can't find the button this page mentions." Apps rename things often. Search the tool's help centre for the feature name, or ask the tool itself where the setting is.
  • "It says I've hit a limit." That's the free plan. Wait for the reset, use a different tool for today, and only upgrade if it keeps happening on work you care about.
  • "The result is generic." Add more context: who it's for, an example of what you like, and what to avoid. Or ask it to ask you questions first. 12 follow-ups that fix weak AI answers has more.
  • "The connection to my Google or Microsoft account fails." Sign out and back in, check you're using the right account (personal or work), and check whether your employer blocks third-party apps.
  • "The Zap didn't run." Check it's switched on, check the trigger app actually received a new entry, and look at the Zap's history for the error message.
  • "The meeting note-taker didn't join." Check the meeting is in the calendar it's connected to, that the link is in the invite, and whether the host has to admit it from a waiting room.

Questions people ask

Do I need all ten tools?

No. Most people need one main assistant and maybe one or two specialists. If your main assistant already does a job well enough, skip the specialist.

Which one should I start with if I'm a complete beginner?

One of the general assistants: Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini. They cover the widest range of jobs. Pick by job helps you choose between them.

Are the free plans really free?

Yes, but with limits on how much you can use them, and some show ads or use your data to improve their models unless you change a setting. Check each tool's privacy settings when you sign up.

Why isn't my favourite tool on the list?

I've only included tools and features I could check on the official pages in October 2026, and kept it to ten. The method works for any tool: one real task, ten minutes, then decide.

What happened to NotebookLM?

Google renamed it Gemini Notebook in July 2026. It's the same product, still at notebooklm.google, and your notebooks also sync with the notebooks in the Gemini app.
