Most weak AI answers come from weak briefs. I spent more than four years on the brand side of influencer marketing, working on 850+ creator deals, and the briefs that went wrong nearly always failed the same way: a fuzzy goal and no example of what good looks like. Prompts fail for the same reasons. This guide gives you the six-box brief I use for creators and for AI, the one line that makes AI ask before it guesses, a full worked example and a template to copy. It works in any AI chat. Reading takes about 15 minutes. Your first proper brief takes about 10.

In short
  • Every box you leave empty gets filled with the most average guess. Fill the boxes yourself.
  • The six boxes: Goal, Audience, Context, Constraints, Format, Example.
  • End with the magic line: "Ask me up to 3 questions before you start."
  • Example is the box that does the most work. Paste something close to what you want.
  • Save the template once. A full brief takes 5 to 10 minutes and saves rounds of rewriting.

Before you start

Any AI chat app works, free plans included. New to them? Start with Claude from zero or ChatGPT from zero and come back. Then have two things ready: a real task you need this week (an email, a plan, a post), and one example of something close to what you want, like an old email that worked.

Why a prompt fails like a bad creator brief

In creator campaigns, most problems start with a vague brief. Send a creator "make something fun about our new product" and you'll get something fun and wrong. The creator isn't bad at the job. They had gaps, and they filled them with their best guess.

AI does the same, only faster and with more confidence. If your prompt doesn't say who the answer is for, it writes for everyone. If it doesn't say how long, it picks a middle length. Every gap gets the most common version of the thing you asked for, which is why so many AI answers feel fine and useless at once. My take

The best creator briefs were short enough to read in two minutes and specific enough that nobody had to guess. Aim for that with AI: a line or two per box.

Box 1: Goal

The goal decides what "good" means. Without one, the AI aims for "sounds nice". With one, it can make choices: what goes first, what to leave out, what to ask the reader to do.

Write one outcome you could check afterwards. A handy shape: "After reading this, [who] will [do what]." If you can't finish that sentence, you're not ready to prompt yet, which is useful to know.

Weak

Write a LinkedIn post about my new job.

Better

Goal: former colleagues should know I now help small shops with their online ads, and the right ones should message me about projects. Likes don't matter much.

Tip

Two goals means two briefs. "Announce my new job and show I'm good at data" pulls the answer in two directions. Pick the one that matters this time.

Box 2: Audience

The audience box changes the words, the length, how much gets explained and which examples get picked. Explaining a pension letter to your teenage nephew and to your accountant are two different jobs.

Cover who they are, what they already know and what they care about right now. Describe one typical person rather than a group. "Busy parents" is a category. "A parent reading on the bus, with five minutes, who has been burned by a subscription they forgot to cancel" is a person.

Weak

Audience: beginners.

Better

Audience: café owners who have never run an online ad. They read on their phone between customers, distrust anything that sounds like a sales pitch, and have heard from a friend that ads "don't work".

Now the AI knows to keep it short and answer the doubt before it comes up.

Heads-up

Describe what your audience needs and knows. Don't ask AI to guess someone's religion, health, politics or background from a name or photo. It can't do that reliably, and it's no fair basis for any decision.

Box 3: Context

Context is everything you know that the AI doesn't: what's happening, what you've tried, what flopped and why now. People skip it because it feels obvious. It's obvious to you, not to the AI.

Weak

We need more newsletter sign-ups.

Better

We run a free weekly newsletter on cheap home cooking. Most sign-ups come from Instagram. A giveaway last spring brought lots of sign-ups who left within a month. We want fewer, better subscribers who actually cook.

Paste, don't describe

Where you can, paste the real thing: the old email, the feedback, the meeting notes. Your summary always loses detail. If you reuse the same background a lot (who you are, how you write), keep it in a file you can attach. Build an about-me folder shows how.

Privacy

Never paste passwords, ID or passport numbers, bank details or other people's private information without their consent. For work documents, remove names and anything confidential first, and check your company's rules on AI tools.

Box 4: Constraints

Constraints are the limits: length, tone, words to avoid, must-include points, language, deadline, budget. In a creator brief this was the ad label, the claims we couldn't make and the posting date. For AI, it's everything you'd otherwise fix by hand afterwards.

Be concrete. "Short" means something different to everyone. "Under 120 words" means one thing.

Weak

Keep it short and professional.

Better

Under 120 words. Warm but not chatty. British English. No exclamation marks. Don't use "excited", "thrilled" or "journey". Must mention the 14 November deadline.

A banned-words list is one of the fastest ways to make AI writing sound less like AI. Keep yours in your template.

Tip

Long rule lists are easy to half-follow, for people and for AI. Put the two or three rules that really matter first and mark them MUST. Leave out rules that don't matter.

Box 5: Format

Format is the shape of the answer: a table with named columns, three options, five bullets, an email with a subject line, a script with timings. If you don't say, you'll usually get paragraphs with headings, which is rarely what you need.

Think about where the answer goes next. Into a spreadsheet? Ask for a table. Into WhatsApp? Short lines, no headings.

Weak

Give me some ideas.

Better

A table with 5 rows. Columns: idea, who it's for, effort (low, medium or high), first step. Then pick the one you'd start with and say why in two sentences.

Tip

Ask for options when you're unsure what you want, and one answer when you know. Three options is a good default: enough to compare, few enough to read properly.

Box 6: Example

This box does the most work. One example shows tone, length, structure and level of detail at once. In creator briefs, a couple of reference videos did more than any amount of "fun but premium, playful but not silly".

An example can be something you made that worked, something you admire (with a note on what you like about it), a rough sketch of the structure, or a bad example clearly labelled "not like this, because it's too salesy". Always say what to copy. Otherwise the AI may copy the topic, the phrases or whole sentences.

Weak

Make it sound like me.

Better

Here are two posts I wrote that people liked. Copy the sentence length, the plain words and the way they open with a specific moment. Don't reuse their phrases or topics.

Heads-up

Use other people's work as a reference for style or structure, never as something to reword and pass off. If voice is what you're after, Make AI write in your voice goes much further.

The magic line

End every brief with this:

Ask me up to 3 questions before you start. If nothing is unclear, say so and go ahead.

AI chat tools are built to answer straight away, even when your request has gaps. This line gives permission to do what a good professional does with a fuzzy brief: ask first. Good creators ask things like "Do you want the product in the first three seconds, or revealed at the end?" before they film, because one answer can save a reshoot. The AI's questions can save you a rewrite.

Why three? Enough to close the big gaps, few enough that you'll answer. The second sentence stops it inventing questions when the brief is already clear.

Answer in one message, numbered to match. Short answers are fine. If you don't know, say "your call" or "give me two versions".

Tip

The questions are a free check on your brief. If the AI asks something you thought was obvious, that box was emptier than you realised. Add the answer to your template for next time.

A full worked example

Say you run a small pottery studio. You've hosted short taster workshops for a couple of years, and now you're launching your first six-week evening course. You need an email to people who came to a taster.

The usual prompt

Write an email about my new pottery course.

What you'll likely get

A cheerful email that could come from any studio. A subject like "Exciting news!", three paragraphs of adjectives, no price, and a vague "get in touch to find out more".

Nothing in it is wrong, but it doesn't make anyone book. Here's the six-box version:

GOAL: Fill the 8 places on my new 6-week evening wheel course, Tuesdays from 4 November. I want people to book or reply with a question, not just read.

AUDIENCE: People who've done one of my 2-hour taster workshops. Mostly adults with day jobs who came as a gift or a night out. Many enjoyed it but said the wheel felt hard. They read email on their phone.

CONTEXT: This is my first multi-week course. After tasters, people often asked how to "actually get good" at the wheel. My last email was a long summer sale with lots of photos and got almost no replies. The course costs €240 for 6 sessions, clay and firing included.

CONSTRAINTS: Under 150 words. Friendly and plain, a little humour is fine. No "exciting news" and no exclamation marks in the subject line. State the price clearly. One link only: [booking link].

FORMAT: 3 subject line options, then the email. Then one line on what you'd change if the first send gets few bookings.

EXAMPLE: Here's an email people liked. Match its tone and length, not its content: [paste your old email]

Ask me up to 3 questions before you start. If nothing is unclear, say so and go ahead.

Expect questions like: "Can you promise something concrete by week six?", "What should people do if Tuesdays don't work?" and "Should I mention that places are limited, or does that feel pushy?" Your reply: "1. Everyone leaves with at least two finished bowls. 2. Join a waitlist for a possible Thursday group. 3. Mention it once, plainly."

Now the AI can open with the exact thing people asked you after tasters, make a promise you're happy with, state the price, and give Tuesday-haters somewhere to go. A first line might read: "After every taster, someone asks me how to actually get good at the wheel. Here's my answer: six Tuesday evenings, and you'll leave with at least two bowls you made yourself." That line only exists because of the Context box and your answer to question 1.

You'll still edit it, but editing a draft that fits takes minutes.

Copy this template

Save this in a note you can reach in two taps. Delete a box that truly doesn't apply, but think twice before deleting Context or Example.

GOAL: [one clear outcome; finish the sentence "after reading this, [who] will [do what]"]

AUDIENCE: [who it's for, what they already know, what they care about right now]

CONTEXT: [background, what you've tried, what didn't work, why now; paste real material where you can]

CONSTRAINTS: [length, tone, language, words to avoid, must-include points, deadline]

FORMAT: [exactly how the answer should be laid out: table and columns, number of options, bullets, email with subject line]

EXAMPLE: [paste something close to what you want and say what to copy (tone, length, structure) and what NOT to copy]

Ask me up to 3 questions before you start. If nothing is unclear, say so and go ahead.

For small everyday tasks, a shorter version is enough:

I need [thing] so that [goal]. It's for [audience]. Keep it [main constraint]. Give it to me as [format]. Here's something close to what I want: [example]. Ask me up to 3 questions before you start.

Variations for writing, research, planning and images

The six boxes stay. What goes into them changes with the job.

Writing

For emails, posts, letters and speeches. Example matters most here.

GOAL: [what the reader should do or feel afterwards]
AUDIENCE: [who reads it and how they know me]
CONTEXT: [what happened before this; paste the email I'm replying to]
CONSTRAINTS: [word limit, tone, words I never use, what I must or must not say]
FORMAT: [email with subject line / post with a strong first line / letter]
EXAMPLE: [something I wrote that sounds like me; copy the rhythm, not the words]
Write a safe version and a bolder one. Ask me up to 3 questions before you start.

Research

For understanding something or comparing options. The key addition is honesty rules, because AI can state wrong things confidently.

GOAL: [the decision or question this should help with]
AUDIENCE: Me. I already know [this] and I'm new to [that].
CONTEXT: [why I'm asking now, what I've read, options I'm considering]
CONSTRAINTS: Separate facts from opinion. Say when you're unsure or when something may have changed recently. Don't invent figures or sources. Name the official sources I should check.
FORMAT: A one-paragraph answer, then a comparison table, then 3 things I should still look into.
Ask me up to 3 questions before you start.
Heads-up

A good brief makes research clearer, not more accurate. AI can be out of date or wrong, especially on prices, laws and recent events. Check anything you'll act on against an official source.

Planning

For trips, events, study plans and moves. Honest context about your time and energy is what makes a plan usable.

GOAL: [what needs to be done, by when]
AUDIENCE: [who follows this plan: me, my family, a team of 3]
CONTEXT: [hours I really have each week, what's done, what went wrong last time]
CONSTRAINTS: [budget, fixed dates, things I won't do, limits like "nothing after 8pm"]
FORMAT: A week-by-week table: week, tasks, time needed, done when. Then the 3 biggest risks and what to do if each happens.
Ask me up to 3 questions before you start.

Images

ChatGPT and Gemini can both make images in the chat (see the ChatGPT images help page and Gemini's image help page). Format becomes shape and style, and Example becomes a reference image you have the right to use.

GOAL: An image for [Instagram post / website header / slide] that makes people [feel or do what].
AUDIENCE: [who will see it]
CONTEXT: [what it's for, brand colours, the mood]
CONSTRAINTS: No text in the image. No logos or real people's faces. Avoid [clichés, e.g. lightbulbs, handshakes].
FORMAT: [square / tall / wide], [photo / flat illustration / watercolour], [lighting and colours].
EXAMPLE: [describe or attach a reference I'm allowed to use; say what to take from it]
First write the image prompt you plan to use and ask me up to 3 questions about it.
Tip

Image tools often start drawing straight away. If yours skips the questions, run the brief in a normal chat, ask it to write the image prompt, then paste that into the image tool.

Save it so you don't retype it

  • Claude: click your initials in the bottom left, open Settings and add standing instructions under "Instructions for Claude" (Claude's help page).
  • ChatGPT: on the web, Settings, then Personalization, then Custom Instructions. In the phone app, Settings, then Customize ChatGPT (OpenAI's help page).
  • Projects: both apps let you group chats in a project with its own instructions and files, a good home for a template you use for one kind of work. See Projects in Claude and ChatGPT.

A useful standing instruction: "When my request is missing something important, ask me up to 3 questions before answering."

Where this goes wrong

  • A brief doesn't fix facts. It shapes the answer. It doesn't stop the AI making things up. Check names, numbers, prices and anything legal.
  • Over-briefing small things. Converting €80 to dollars doesn't need six boxes. Use the full brief when the result matters or the first answer keeps missing.
  • Boxes that fight. "Under 100 words" plus "cover all 12 points" can't both happen. When the output is odd, check your brief for contradictions first.
  • Stopping at the first answer. Even a great brief gets a first draft. 12 follow-ups that fix weak AI answers has the replies I use next.

When it gets stuck

It ignored the questions line. Put the magic line last, on its own line. If it still answers straight away, reply: "Stop. Before you write anything, ask me your 3 questions."

The answer is still generic. Context or Example is probably thin. Add one real detail only you know, such as what flopped last time, and paste a real example.

Right content, wrong shape. Fix Format only: "Same content, as a table with these columns: [columns]."

It keeps breaking a rule. Move that rule to the top of Constraints as a MUST, and ask it to check its answer against the rules before showing it.

It copied your example too closely. Reply: "Keep the tone and structure. Change every sentence and the topic."

Your action plan

  • Copy the six-box template into a note on your phone.
  • Pick one real task for this week and fill all six boxes. Give it about 10 minutes.
  • Run it with the magic line and answer the questions in one numbered reply.
  • Compare the result with what a one-line prompt gives you.
  • Add the questions it asked to your template, so next time you answer them up front.
  • Save the magic line in your app's standing instructions.

Questions people ask

Isn't this a lot of typing for one answer?

For a quick question, yes, so skip it. For anything you'll send, publish or act on, ten minutes of briefing usually beats three rounds of "no, not like that". With the template saved, it's mostly filling gaps.

Does this work in every AI app?

Yes. The boxes are about what you tell the AI, not how one app works, so they work in Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and others. Image tools sometimes skip the questions; see the image tip above.

What if I don't have an example?

Describe one, or give a bad example and say why it's wrong. Or ask the AI for three very different short samples, pick the closest, and use that as your example.
