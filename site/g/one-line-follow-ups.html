Most people read the first AI answer, decide it's a bit flat, and leave. That first answer is a draft. In my experience the good one is usually reply #3, after you've told the AI what's wrong with the first. This page gives you 12 one-line follow-ups, grouped by the problem they fix, with why each one works and a quick before and after. They work in Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini or any other chat app. Reading takes about 12 minutes. Using one takes five seconds.

In short
  • The first answer is a draft. Name what's wrong with it, then send the matching follow-up.
  • 12 follow-ups for 6 problems: too long, too generic, too sure of itself, too agreeable, hard to use, and stiff.
  • The one I use most: "That's too generic. Ask me 3 questions about my situation first, then rewrite it."
  • Stay in the same chat and send one or two at a time.
  • Copy the cheat sheet at the bottom into your notes app.

How to use these follow-ups

  1. Name the problem first. Before replying, say in one word what's wrong: too long, generic, overconfident, too agreeable, hard to use, stiff. The headings below match those problems.
  2. Send the matching line. Every prompt box starts with the short follow-up. The sentences after it are a stronger version for when the short line isn't enough. Copy just the first sentence or the whole box.
  3. Stay in the same chat. The AI needs to see what it wrote. A follow-up in a new chat has nothing to fix.
  4. One or two at a time. Ask for five changes at once and you'll get a muddle. Fix the biggest problem, read again, then fix the next.
  5. Fill the brackets. Anything in [square brackets] is yours to replace.

When it's too long

1. "Half the words, same meaning."

Fixes: answers padded with a warm-up, repeated points and a summary of what you just read.

"Shorter" is vague, so you often get the same answer with a few words trimmed. "Half" is a target the AI can measure, and "same meaning" tells it to keep the content and cut the padding. It's the fastest edit you'll ever make.

Half the words, same meaning. Keep every name, number, date and action step. Cut the warm-up, any point you've already made and the closing summary.
Before

Great question! Starting a balcony garden can be a really rewarding experience. There are several important factors to consider before you begin, including sunlight, the size of your space and the climate where you live...

After

Check two things first: how many hours of direct sun the balcony gets, and how much weight it can take. Then pick plants that suit both.

2. "What would you cut first?"

Fixes: plans, lists and reports with too many points to act on.

This makes the AI rank its own points by importance. You see its reasoning, and you stay in charge of what goes. It's especially good for plans, where twelve steps usually means nothing gets done.

What would you cut first? Rank every point from least to most important, cross out the bottom third, and give me one line on why each cut is safe. Then show me the shorter version.
Before

A 14-point plan to get fitter, from "buy a heart-rate monitor" to "meal prep every Sunday" to "join a running club".

After

Three points that matter (walk daily, two short strength sessions a week, a fixed bedtime) and a list of what was cut, with why.

When it's too generic

3. "That's too generic. Ask me 3 questions about my situation first, then rewrite it."

Fixes: advice that could apply to anyone. Track your spending. Set clear goals. Get enough sleep.

A generic answer usually means the AI was missing context about you. This line flips the conversation so the AI interviews you. Three questions is enough to get the details that matter and few enough that you'll answer them. It's the follow-up version of the magic line in Brief AI the way you'd brief a pro, and if I could keep only one line on this page, it would be this one.

That's too generic. Ask me 3 questions about my situation first, then rewrite it.
Before

To save money each month, track your spending, make a budget and cut unnecessary expenses.

After

(After three questions about your bills and habits.) Your easiest win is the two streaming services and the gym you said you haven't used since summer. Cancel those this week, then look at your phone contract, which ends in March.

4. "Give me the version an expert would write."

Fixes: answers that are correct but beginner-level, or read like a school essay.

This moves the target from the average version of an answer to an expert's version. Naming the kind of expert helps even more, because a senior recruiter writes a CV line differently from a careers blog. Experts also skip the obvious, so the answer usually gets tighter.

Give me the version an expert would write. Pick the kind of expert who knows this best (for example [a senior recruiter / a professional chef / an experienced teacher]), tell me who you picked, then rewrite it the way they would. Skip anything they'd consider obvious.
Before

Your CV should highlight your skills and experience in a clear and professional way.

After

Open each job with your strongest result, not your duties. Delete every line that starts with "responsible for". Cut anything older than ten years unless it's directly relevant.

Heads-up

"The version an expert would write" is still AI writing. For health, money, tax or legal questions it isn't professional advice. Use it to get a sharper draft, then check with a real professional when it matters.

5. "Explain it with an example from [my life or job]."

Fixes: explanations that make sense while you read them and vanish when you try to use them.

A concrete example forces the AI to apply the idea, which exposes gaps in the explanation. And an example from your own world sticks, because you can picture it.

Explain it with an example from [my life or job, e.g. running a small bakery / working shifts in a hospital / planning a family holiday]. Walk through it step by step with realistic details, then tell me the one thing I should remember.
Before

Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative you give up when making a decision.

After

If you spend Saturday morning baking for a market stall, the opportunity cost is the café shift you'd otherwise work. The stall has to beat that shift's pay, and your tiredness, to be worth it.

When it's too sure of itself

6. "What did you assume that I didn't tell you?"

Fixes: confident answers built on guesses you can't see.

AI fills gaps quietly. It decides how many people you're cooking for, what your budget is, what country you live in, and it doesn't tell you. This question makes those guesses visible so you can correct the wrong ones. The list is often the most useful part, because it shows you what you forgot to say.

What did you assume that I didn't tell you? List each assumption, say whether it changed your answer a lot or a little, and what you'd change if it's wrong. Then wait for my corrections.
Before

A weekly meal plan with salmon twice and a 40-minute dinner every night.

After

"I assumed you cook for two, eat fish, cook every evening and have about 40 minutes." You reply "One person, no fish, 20 minutes max" and get a plan you'll actually follow.

7. "Which part are you least sure about?"

Fixes: answers full of facts, figures and rules you can't easily check.

This tells you where to check first. You won't verify every sentence, so it helps to know which ones are shakiest. Rules, prices, dates and anything recent are the usual suspects.

Which part are you least sure about? Mark each claim in your answer as solid, probably right, or a guess. For anything that isn't solid, tell me exactly what to check and which official source to check it with.
Before

A confident paragraph about tenant rights, with a notice period and a deposit rule stated as fact.

After

The same answer with the deposit rule marked "probably right, check your local tenancy rules" and the notice period marked "a guess, check your contract".

Heads-up

An AI's sense of its own certainty isn't reliable. It can be confidently wrong about which parts are wrong. Use this question to decide where to look first, never as a replacement for checking.

When it agrees too much or repeats itself

8. "Now argue against it."

Fixes: answers that cheer on whatever you said.

AI chat tools tend to go along with the way you frame things. Tell it you're excited about a plan and it will usually help with the plan. Asking it to argue the other side gets you the objections a sensible friend would raise, before reality raises them for you.

Now argue against it. Be the most sensible sceptic you can. Give me the 3 strongest reasons this is a bad idea, what would have to be true for it to fail, and the early warning signs I should watch for.
Before

Turning your candle hobby into a business is a brave move! Here's a launch plan...

After

Three risks: it's a crowded market where buyers compare on price; you haven't sold to anyone outside friends yet; and your costs per candle aren't known. Test sales at one market before buying stock.

9. "Give me 3 options that are actually different."

Fixes: "three options" that are one idea in three outfits.

When you ask for options, they tend to cluster around the most likely answer. Saying "actually different", and naming what they should differ on, pushes them apart.

Give me 3 options that are actually different. Make them differ on [cost / effort / risk / style]: one safe, one bold, and one I probably haven't thought of. For each, one line on who it suits.
Before

Birthday ideas: dinner at an Italian restaurant, dinner at a Thai restaurant, dinner at a tapas bar.

After

Cook together at home with three friends; a day trip by train to a lake; or a "yes day" where the birthday person sets every plan.

When it's hard to use

10. "Show this as a table."

Fixes: comparisons and options buried in paragraphs.

A table forces the AI to line up the same facts for every option, so gaps show immediately: an empty cell is a missing fact. It's also easier to scan and to paste into a spreadsheet.

Show this as a table. One row per option, with columns for [price, length of commitment, main benefit, the catch]. Keep each cell under 10 words and add a final row with your recommendation.
Before

Four paragraphs comparing three phone contracts, with the prices and terms scattered through the text.

After

A three-row table: monthly price, contract length, data, the catch. The cheapest one turns out to have a price rise after a year.

11. "Turn this into steps I can do in the next hour."

Fixes: advice that's right but leaves you unsure what to do first.

A time limit forces the AI to pick the first real actions and make them small. It turns something you read into something you do, today.

Turn this into steps I can do in the next hour. Number them, put a time next to each, make the first one take under 5 minutes, and tell me what "done" looks like at the end of the hour.
Before

Decluttering starts with a mindset shift. Think about what you truly value and how your space makes you feel...

After

1. Get two bags, one for bin and one for donate (2 min). 2. Clear the kitchen counter only (15 min). 3. Empty one drawer onto the table and put back only what you used this month (20 min)...

When it sounds like a robot

12. "Make it sound like a person talking."

Fixes: stiff, formal, wordy text you'd never say out loud.

Spoken language uses short sentences, common words and contractions. Asking for "a person talking" pulls in all of that in one go. Read the result aloud: if you'd stumble over a sentence, ask again. For a voice that sounds like you specifically, see Make AI write in your voice.

Make it sound like a person talking. Short sentences, everyday words, contractions. Remove anything I wouldn't say out loud to a friend, and drop words like "additionally", "furthermore" and "ensure".
Before

I am writing to inform you that, due to unforeseen circumstances, I will unfortunately be unable to attend Thursday's meeting.

After

Sorry, something's come up and I can't make Thursday. Could we move it to next week?

Stacking them: why reply #3 is the good one

The follow-ups work best in a short sequence. Here's a typical one.

You ask for a plan to learn some Spanish before a holiday. Reply 1 is a tidy eight-week plan that would suit anyone. You send: "That's too generic. Ask me 3 questions about my situation first, then rewrite it." It asks your level, how much time you have each day, and what you'll actually need Spanish for. You answer: total beginner, 15 minutes on the commute, ordering food and asking directions.

Reply 2 is a plan built around restaurant and street phrases, sized for the commute. Good, but long. You send: "Turn this into steps I can do in the next hour." Reply 3 is today's first session: ten phrases, a way to practise them out loud, and a two-minute self-test. That's the one you use.

Two or three follow-ups is usually enough. If you're on reply #8 and still unhappy, the problem is the brief, not the follow-ups. Start a new chat with a better one.

Make them work better

Fix the brief first. Follow-ups repair a weak answer. A good brief prevents one. Five minutes on the six-box brief means fewer follow-ups.

Keep them one tap away. Paste the cheat sheet below into your phone's notes app. Many phone keyboards also let you save text shortcuts, so typing a short code like "tg" expands to the full too-generic line. Look in your keyboard settings.

Make the good habits permanent. Some follow-ups can live in your standing instructions so you need them less. In Claude, open Settings and use "Instructions for Claude" (help page). In ChatGPT on the web, go to Settings, then Personalization, then Custom Instructions (help page). A good line to add: "Keep answers short. When my request is missing details, ask up to 3 questions first. Tell me when you're unsure."

Checked in October 2026. AI apps change fast: if a button has moved, search the app's help pages for the feature name.

Say what to keep. "Keep the second paragraph, redo the rest" gets a better result than "try again", because the AI knows what worked.

Where follow-ups don't help

  • Wrong facts stay wrong. If the AI doesn't know something, asking again can give you a different wrong answer. Check anything important against an official source.
  • Pushback isn't proof. AI tends to give way when you disagree, even when its first answer was right. Ask for reasons, not surrender: "Explain why, and tell me if you still think your first answer was correct."
  • Long chats drift. If you're going in circles, open a new chat, paste the best version so far, and brief it properly.
  • Not professional advice. For money, health, legal or tax questions, follow-ups sharpen a draft. They don't replace a qualified person when the stakes are real.

Questions people ask

Why not just write a better first prompt?

You should, and the six-box brief shows how. But even a great brief produces a draft, and you often only know what you want once you see what you don't. Follow-ups are how you get from there to done.

Do these work in every AI app?

Yes. They're plain instructions, so they work in Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot and others. Some apps follow length and format instructions more closely than others, so if one line doesn't land, try the longer version in its prompt box.

Should I keep going or start a new chat?

Keep going while each reply gets better. Start fresh when it's circling the same idea, mixing up details from earlier, or you've changed your mind about what you want. Paste the best version so far into the new chat.

Why does the AI change its answer as soon as I push back?

Chat tools are built to be helpful, and that can tip into agreeing too easily. When you disagree, ask it to explain its reasoning and say whether it still stands by the first answer. Then judge the reasons, not the tone.
